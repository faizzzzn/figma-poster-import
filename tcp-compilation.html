Chapter 01

The problem, documented

Failure analysis Corrosion research 4 photos · deck p. 40

“Moisture is a major cause of hearing aid damage, reducing device lifespan and performance. Existing solutions often fail to provide safe, reliable, and efficient moisture removal for sensitive electronic components.”

The project opened with failure analysis, not form. Corroded receiver terminals pulled from devices across markets were documented against a published reference: “A comparative study on corrosion failure analysis of hearing aid devices from different markets” — Yadav, 2023 — Materials and Corrosion (Wiley).

Where the moisture comes from: sweat · humid weather · condensation from walking out of a fully air-conditioned room · accidental splashes · and earwax binding with moisture to choke components. Hearing aids live in the ear canal — a high-moisture environment — packed with microelectronics (microphone, volume control, processor, battery, amplifier) that water attacks daily.

What users reported: battery issues from moisture · earwax-plus-moisture blocking components · static noise and feedback · device malfunction and shortened lifespan · difficulty keeping up maintenance routines · discomfort during sleep.

“To achieve — a compact and user-friendly dehumidification system that protects hearing aids effectively without exposing them to harmful heat?”

Chapter 02

Market study — five dryers, one gap

5 products compared deck p. 43 USA · India/China

Five incumbent dryers were compared across cycle time, drying method and India street price — from a ₹200 passive jar to ₹20,000 imports. The pattern: every product either cooks the aid or leaves it damp.

Market products leave relative humidity at 25–30% against a <10% RH target — figures from the project deck, not independently verified published specs. Unregulated heat corrodes circuits and degrades batteries, and no two products share a common drying benchmark, so moisture claims can't be compared.

Chapter 03

Desiccant science — why sieve 3A

Bead tests deck pp. 46–48 WiseSorb 3A

Molecular sieves are zeolites with 3–10 ångström pores — adsorbents that trap molecules by size (H2O, CO2, N2), used for extreme drying and gas purification. Bench tests used WiseSorb 3A, rechargeable.

Why the sieve won: it adsorbs aggressively even at low humidity, has extremely high water affinity, and rapidly pulls moisture down toward 10% RH. Silica gel absorbs gradually and works at moderate-to-high humidity — but its curve plateaus and declines at saturation. The bead tests photographed the difference, and that result is what made the cartridge regenerable-by-design instead of disposable.

How Might We

8 questions Framing the concept phase

Eight solution-free questions carried the research into concepting — kept deliberately open so the answers could come from the bench, not from assumptions.

  1. Prevent condensation buildup inside a closed drying system?

  2. Maintain performance within safe temperature limits at all times?

  3. Ensure safe operation under extreme conditions like overheating, leaks, or saturation?

  4. Remove moisture from deep internal regions without damaging sensitive electronics or outer casing?

  5. Design a system that integrates seamlessly into a user’s daily routine?

  6. Minimize user interaction while maximizing effectiveness?

  7. Make the device portable, intuitive, and easy to operate with one hand?

  8. Ensure the product feels effortless and reliable for elderly users?

Chapter 05

Feature integration — six technologies, one system

deck p. 49 4 diagrams Physics corrected 2026-10-02

The six technologies below are how the research answered the HMWs. The physics was reviewed and corrected after the deck was drawn — the corrected version is the only one recorded here.

T.01

RF dielectric heating — 13.56 MHz

A microcontroller-driven 13.56 MHz ISM-band RF generator drives a dielectric field between parallel plates. At this frequency heating is dominated by ionic conduction rather than molecular rotation — so salty sweat (~1.8 g/L NaCl, the actual contaminant killing hearing aids) heats preferentially. It does not heat “only water molecules”: everything lossy in the field warms somewhat.

Two genuine advantages: deep penetration (λ ≈ 22 m reaches internal regions microwaves can’t), and negligible direct pickup by the aid’s own electronics — a 2 cm device is electrically tiny at this wavelength, a poor antenna.

T.02

Faraday cage — the outer EMC shield

The layered copper / polyester / polypropylene shield is the product’s outer electromagnetic-compatibility enclosure: it contains RF emissions so the device is safe and doesn’t interfere with its surroundings. It does not sit around the hearing aid — a cage there would block the RF field from ever reaching the moisture. The aid sits in the field, between the plates.

T.03

Molecular sieve cartridge — Grade 3A, reusable

A regenerable Grade-3A sieve cartridge captures what the field liberates, fed by the channeled airflow system. Rechargeable by design — the desiccant direction the bead tests validated.

T.04

UV-C sanitisation

Germicidal UV-C in the open-lid cradle sanitises the aids while they dry — one nightly ritual, two jobs done.

T.05

Channeled airflow

Controlled air circulation carries liberated vapour through the sieve cartridge. Without airflow plus desiccant, evaporated moisture would simply recondense inside the case.

T.06

Feedback loop — temperature-guarded

Microcontroller feedback governs modes and on/off, with a temperature feedback loop enforcing safe limits. “No harmful heat” comes from this loop — not from the frequency itself. Power: 3.7 V Li-ion/Li-Po, 3000–5000 mAh, USB-C charging (project values).

The system narrative

RF liberates the moisture → airflow carries it → the sieve captures it. The trio is the product; each stage needs the other two.

Physics reviewed; bench validation pending. Battery and RF figures are project values from the student deck, not validated performance.

Chapter 10

References — case studies & papers

Independently researched 2026-10-02

Every claim the project leans on should trace to something real. These are the papers and studies behind the corrosion story, the RF mechanism, and the desiccant choice — researched independently and linked for reading.

Corrosion & failure analysis

R.01

Yadav, A., & Ambat, R. (2023). A comparative study on corrosion failure analysis of hearing aid devices from different markets. Materials and Corrosion, 74(1), 159–173.

Why it matters: root-cause analysis of real failed hearing aids across markets — KOH electrolyte leakage in tropical humidity, sweat chlorides, galvanic corrosion of gold-plated contacts, coating delamination. The corrosion photo set in Chapter 01 traces to this work. (DTU + WS Audiology.)

doi.org/10.1002/maco.202213420 ↗
R.02

Yadav, A., Gupta, K. K., Ambat, R., & Christensen, M. L. (2021). Statistical analysis of corrosion failures in hearing aid devices from tropical regions. Engineering Failure Analysis, 130, 105758.

Why it matters: the statistical backbone — corrosion failure rates specifically in tropical regions, i.e. the climate case for a dryer designed for Indian humidity.

doi.org/10.1016/j.engfailanal.2021.105758 ↗
R.03

Asikainen, M., Yadav, A., Freels, B., & Ambat, R. (2024). A systematic corrosion failure analysis of Li-ion powered hearing aids from various markets. Corrosion Engineering, Science and Technology.

Why it matters: the newer battery chemistry — passive components, solder joints and galvanic charging contacts fail most; causes are chloride ions, high humidity, flux residues and insufficient coating protection.

doi.org/10.1177/1478422X241275299 ↗
R.04

Rao, J. M., Yadav, A., Conseil-Gudla, H., & Ambat, R. (2022). Synergetic effect of temperature and humidity on the leakage of KOH electrolyte and related reliability of zinc-air batteries. Frontiers in Materials, 9.

Why it matters: the mechanism behind battery-driven corrosion — heat plus humidity drives KOH electrolyte leakage, which is hygroscopic and corrosive to the surrounding electronics.

RF dielectric heating

R.05

Piyasena, P., Dussault, C., Koutchma, T., Ramaswamy, H. S., & Awuah, G. B. (2007). Radio frequency heating of foods: Principles, applications and related properties — A review. Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition, 43(6), 587–606.

Why it matters: the physics reference — RF heating principles including ionic conduction, the mechanism ARID's 13.56 MHz stage relies on.

doi.org/10.1080/10408690390251129 ↗
R.06

Review of dielectric drying of foods and agricultural products (2011), Vol. 4, No. 1.

Why it matters: a dielectric-drying survey — dipole rotation plus ionic conduction as the combined polarization mechanisms, and the 13.56 MHz ISM-band allocation ARID uses.

idc-online.com — review PDF ↗

Desiccant science

R.07

Desiccant films made of low-density polyethylene with dispersed silica gel — water vapor absorption, permeability and mechanical properties. Materials (2019), 12, 2304.

Why it matters: the isotherm evidence — molecular sieves absorb water vapour down to a few percent RH with a sharp low-RH uptake, while silica gel needs >50% RH to condense. The published justification for Grade-3A over silica gel.
